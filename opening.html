Pulling the Sycophancy Thread

Dorit Cohen · 11 October 2026

What competent-looking output can hide: silent failures in a deployed system, then in the raters that judged it — and what a task-oriented design does to sycophancy

Summary. This started as a narrow measurement of sycophancy across the production conversations of Syncca, a relational-guidance system I built on Claude Sonnet 4.6. A person usually arrives flooded, sure the fault is the other's; the work is to move them from that stance — to see their own part, to hold the absent other as a distinct person, and then to choose how to act. None of that can be done by agreeing with them: to endorse the one-sided account is to fail the task. There are no instructions against sycophancy in the system. While the usual approach prompts or trains against it directly, this raised a testable question: when a task is designed so that successful execution requires holding the user's premise open, can sycophancy fall as a by-product of doing the task? Whether that task is itself a good one to give a model is beyond this article's scope.

The prompt derives from Murray Bowen's differentiation of self and the work of David Schnarch. Beneath it sits a Lexicon: a structured progression of applied concepts and tools, gathered over two decades of working with people on their relationships. A per-turn Gate detects whether the user is emotionally flooded or regulated. The two work together, turn by turn: containing and staying curious while the user is flooded, and drawing on the Lexicon only once the user is ready to take it in.
